## English version

An introduction to AI can begin with how to test a model as well as how to prompt one. On October 26, Google Research and the Stanford Accelerator for Learning [introduced Polar Peaks](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/education/ai-quests-polar-peaks/), the third interactive lesson in AI Quests for students aged eleven to fourteen. Participants take the role of researchers working with neural connections. It is a science-inspired educational scenario, rather than an announcement that a complete human-brain map has been produced.

The story concerns a game character's memory loss. With virtual mentors, students undertake neuron-scanning and mapping tasks, data cleaning and model testing. This places attention on more than the output alone: preparing inputs and checking results also matter. The announcement nevertheless describes the creators' educational design. It does not establish how successfully every learner will understand those ideas in a real classroom, where prior knowledge, teacher support and the surrounding discussion can influence what students take away.

The setting draws on connectomics, the study of connections in nervous systems. The authors relate it to Google's long-running research and work mapping the fruit-fly brain. These scales should remain distinct. An actual scientific project supplies inspiration, while a game introduces an approach. Completing the quest is not equivalent to professional analysis of neural data or a clinical finding about an individual's memory. Moving from an educational example to research requires different evidence, expertise and conditions than the scenario provides.

Google reports more than four hundred thousand completed quests across the series and localization into ten languages. Completions are not the same as unique students, measured learning gains or an independent assessment of educational effectiveness. Nor should the cumulative total be assigned entirely to the newly introduced Polar Peaks. The series already contained quests about flood forecasting and eye-disease detection. The unit being counted and the set of lessons covered by a statistic both matter when interpreting its scale.

Teacher-training modules are being released alongside the initiative. At the time of the announcement, modules for the first two quests were accessible, while the third quest's module was expected the following month. Not every teacher resource therefore arrived at the same time as the new game. Classroom use also involves pedagogical context: what to discuss afterward and how to connect the activity to the curriculum. ### Why it matters

Data-cleaning and testing tasks offer a more concrete introduction to AI literacy than interface familiarity alone. Keeping people involved in verification is a useful part of that framing. Evaluating the lesson's educational value will require examining which ideas learners understand and can use afterward. Popularity and an appealing scientific story may generate interest, while the learning outcome still needs its own observation and discussion with teachers rather than being inferred from the existence of a game.
