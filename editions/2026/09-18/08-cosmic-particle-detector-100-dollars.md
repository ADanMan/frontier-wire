Устройство выросло из студенческого физического проекта — обычно такие вещи остаются в рамках курсовой и забываются. Дешевизна здесь не мелочь, а суть истории. ## English version

An invisible stream of particles from space passes through every one of us, all the time. You can't feel it — but you no longer need a million-dollar lab to see it. [ScienceDaily reports](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/09/260917003725.htm) that a pocket-sized detector costing roughly $100 can reveal this constant rain of cosmic particles hitting Earth and passing straight through our bodies.

The device grew out of a student physics project — the kind of thing that usually stays a class assignment and gets forgotten. This one didn't: it's now being used in major experiments, flown on balloon missions, and considered for future spacecraft. Going from a classroom build to a tool serious research groups actually rely on is unusual in itself — instruments like this typically cost orders of magnitude more and require specialized manufacturing.

The low price isn't a footnote here — it's the whole point. Cosmic rays, high-energy particles arriving from deep space, are normally studied with expensive, bulky detectors available to a handful of labs. A hundred-dollar device changes that math: a school, a small university, or an independent researcher can now afford one. This is a case where cutting the price doesn't dumb down the science — it widens who gets to do it, from classrooms to high-altitude balloons drifting through the stratosphere.

### Why it matters

When the cost of entry for serious cosmic-ray measurement drops from a lab budget to the price of a smartphone, the science stops being confined to a few well-funded institutes — and data that used to trickle in from a handful of sites can start pouring in from thousands at once.
