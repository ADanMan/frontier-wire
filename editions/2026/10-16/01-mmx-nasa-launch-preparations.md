## English version

In its [October 15 announcement](https://science.nasa.gov/directorates/smd/planetary-science-division/nasa-to-help-unlock-mysteries-of-mars-moons-in-upcoming-mission/), NASA describes its role in Japan's MMX mission ahead of a planned launch. JAXA's spacecraft is intended to investigate Phobos and Deimos and bring material from Phobos back to Earth. The published schedule targets October 19 in the eastern United States, already October 20 in Japan. At the time of this edition, that is a plan rather than a completed launch.

The new development concerns specific mission preparations, not an announcement that the moons' origin has been settled. NASA lists the MEGANE spectrograph, a pneumatic sampler, and deep-space communications and navigation support. Measurements near the target and eventual laboratory analysis should provide different kinds of evidence. Their expected value should not be confused with results already obtained.

The spectrograph was developed by the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory as part of NASA’s stated contribution. MEGANE is designed to infer surface elemental composition from neutrons and gamma rays. The pneumatic instrument is intended to collect surface material using nitrogen pulses, complementing Japan's sampler for material from deeper down. NASA also expects to receive and curate roughly 15% of the returned sample. These describe responsibilities and objectives, rather than a scientific programme that has already been carried out.

Why devote such attention to small moons? The agency's account retains two competing explanations: capture of ancient asteroids, or formation from debris expelled by an impact on Mars. The mission should help distinguish those histories. The underlying comparison matters: external resemblance alone is insufficient when different processes could produce similar-looking objects.

The chain of investigation will be lengthy. NASA gives a planned Mars arrival in August 2027, sampling no earlier than December 2028, and return to Earth in 2031. This is the announced timeline. It explains why the next milestone, launch, matters operationally without supplying an answer to the central scientific question.

A useful way to follow the mission is to keep instrument readiness, departure, measurements at the target and analysis of returned material separate. Each stage has its own test of success. Completing the first would make later work possible, rather than establish that the entire undertaking has succeeded. That distinction also prevents a calendar of intended milestones from being read as a list of achievements.

### Why it matters

MMX connects remote measurements with the prospect of laboratory investigation of Phobos material. The immediate news is NASA's contribution and the approaching launch target. The more consequential scientific result comes later: comparing physical samples with explanations that currently remain unresolved.
