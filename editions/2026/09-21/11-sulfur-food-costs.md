## English version

NPR's [Planet Money traces an unexpected economic chain](https://www.npr.org/sections/planet-money/2026/09/18/nx-s1-5974103/what-expensive-sulfur-means-for-your-dinner-table): oil supplies are falling, and sulfur — a byproduct of oil refining — is falling with them. Less oil refined means less sulfur reaches the market, and its price climbs.

On the surface, that sounds like a niche story for the chemical industry. But sulfur feeds into sulfuric acid, which is used heavily in fertilizer production, so a pricier sulfur market adds cost pressure all the way down the chain, from the field to the checkout counter. That's why the show frames the question directly: what does expensive sulfur mean for your dinner table.

This is a classic Planet Money move: take a raw material nobody thinks about day to day and show how price swings in that narrow market eventually reach grocery prices a few steps later. There's no simple one-to-one link between oil prices and food prices here, but it's not a mechanism worth ignoring when thinking about where food inflation heads next.

### Why it matters

The chain from less oil to less sulfur to pricier fertilizer to pricier food is a good example of how decisions in one corner of the economy that looks unrelated to groceries can still show up on supermarket price tags months later.
