## English version

A decade-long experiment at the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) has [produced a new measurement of the gravitational constant G — and it unexpectedly disagrees with another leading result](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/09/260920032049.htm), ScienceDaily reports. The discrepancy is tiny, but for one of nature's most fundamental constants, even a small mismatch means a 225-year-old physics puzzle remains unresolved.

The gravitational constant was first measured by Henry Cavendish back in 1798, and labs around the world have kept getting slightly different values from each other ever since — unlike other fundamental constants, which have converged toward a single agreed number as instruments improved. G is the stubborn exception: the more precisely it's measured, the more the disagreement persists.

The key detail of the NIST experiment is that it was run "blind": the result was sealed and left unopened for ten years while the work continued, specifically to rule out researchers unconsciously nudging the data toward an expected number. Opening the envelope didn't settle the dispute — it just confirmed that the gravity mismatch is real, not a single lab's mistake.

### Why it matters

The gravitational constant is one of the few fundamental quantities in nature that stubbornly refuses to converge across independent measurements, and NIST's new decade-long blind experiment only confirmed the old anomaly instead of resolving it.
