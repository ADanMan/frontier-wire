## English version

The stereotype of bland British food has stuck around for decades: jellied eels, overcooked vegetables, roast beef and pudding held up as the epitome of culinary dullness next to other nations' cuisines. But [historical research shows 18th-century British food was complex, multicultural, and genuinely flavorful](https://phys.org/news/2026-09-british-food-complex-multicultural-flavorful.html), phys.org reports.

The stereotype's staying power is more interesting than the stereotype itself: it persists not because sources back it up, but because it became part of national self-description — "boring food" turned into a cultural joke about the British that gets repeated far more often than it gets checked. Historians looking at actual 18th-century cookbooks and food practices find a very different picture: a variety of spices, the imprint of the empire's trade networks, a culinary complexity that doesn't sit well with boiled cabbage as the national dish.

The gap between the myth and the sources is a familiar story for national food identity: what a country says about its own cooking often reveals more about its self-deprecating humor than about what people actually ate.
