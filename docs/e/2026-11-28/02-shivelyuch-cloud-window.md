## English version

NASA Earth Observatory has published a [satellite view of Shivelyuch](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/shivelyuch-socked-in/) in which clouds cover lower slopes while leaving the caldera and parts of the upper volcano visible. The Image of the Day is dated November 27; Landsat 9 acquired the image on November 8, 2026. It enters our November 28 evening digest as a new presentation of an earlier observation. The picture is not an operational report of the volcano's condition at the moment someone reads this story. Acquisition and publication dates remain explicitly separate.

Shivelyuch stands on Russia's Kamchatka Peninsula. NASA gives the summit elevation as 3,283 metres and describes a roughly nine-kilometre-wide upper caldera open to the south. During acquisition, clouds remained below the summit, allowing Landsat 9's OLI instrument to show the caldera and northern upper slopes. The opening provides a useful view of the mountain's structure. It does not mean that optical observations are continuously possible or that the surrounding area was equally visible throughout the scene.

A dark area within the caldera marks a lava dome. Earth Observatory explains that these structures form as viscous magma emerges and can undergo cycles of growth and collapse. Some collapses trigger fast-moving flows of hot ash and rock. That mechanism explains the kind of feature shown; it does not establish that the picture captures a particular collapse in progress. We have not measured material velocity from a single image or treated the dark patch as an independent measurement of present hazard.

NASA's account of Kamchatka Volcanic Eruption Response Team reports says effusive eruptions and explosive activity had continued for months by November. Satellites regularly detected thermal anomalies despite frequent cloud cover. For the day of acquisition, the source reports a plume of resuspended ash drifting 120 kilometres eastward. The figure belongs to that dated observation, rather than describing ash location today. A plume's horizontal extent should also not be confused with its height above the volcano or assumed to establish an ongoing flight disruption.

Earth Observatory notes that other explosions can send ash several kilometres upward, allowing transport over long distances. It mentions a July episode carrying ash toward Alaska and flight cancellations reported by the press. That is historical context, not an assertion of new cancellations on November 28. ### Why it matters

A satellite picture reveals terrain and the limits of observation at the same time. A break in cloud cover exposes details of an active volcano without turning the image into a continuous event record. Knowing the acquisition date, instrument and provenance of accompanying information helps separate visible structure from an explanation of its mechanism and from operational conclusions requiring additional evidence.
